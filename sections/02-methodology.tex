\section{Review Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

The review examines how computational assistance participates in life-science visual analysis, which tasks it supports, and what evidence establishes its consequences for scientific work. Its scope follows the relationship among the data, the visual representation, the computational contribution, and the scientist’s role. A system’s use of an AI method or an immersive display alone does not establish that relationship.

The review protocol separates eligibility from classification and synthesis. Eligibility determines whether a paper reports a qualifying system. The five-task lens, assistance modes, and visualization environments introduced in Section 3 describe the work supported by eligible systems and provide a basis for comparison. The locus of analytic agency guides interpretation of documented computational interventions and human responsibilities; it is not an additional eligibility criterion or categorical score. This separation allows systems with different technologies and levels of automation to be examined under the same inclusion rules.

The following subsections specify scope and eligibility, sources and retrieval, record reconciliation, screening and human decisions, coding and synthesis, and reproducibility. The eligibility rules below follow the frozen coding rubric, version 1.0.0, dated 30 August 2026. These rules define the review boundary; completed retrieval, screening, and validation require separate reporting of their procedures and results.

The screening unit is a paper. When a paper describes multiple systems, at least one identified system must jointly satisfy all seven eligibility criteria below. Evidence for unrelated systems within a paper cannot be combined to establish eligibility. Qualifying systems and their computational mechanisms provide the units for subsequent comparison, but individual mechanisms are not counted as separate corpus members.

E1—Life-science application. The reported system must be applied to analysis, understanding, monitoring, decision-making, discovery, or scientific practice in a life-science or closely related domain. This includes biomedical and clinical science, health, neuroscience, ecology, marine science, biology, and bioinformatics. Clinical and surgical applications follow the same rules as other applications. A surgical system may qualify through an interactive analytic workflow; training, teleoperation, procedural guidance, or presentation alone does not establish that relationship.

E2—Relational, derived-structure, or multiscale relevance. The system must analyze explicit networks or relationships, or relationships derived from spatial, temporal, multivariate, image-derived, lineage, similarity, or other multiscale life-science data. Eligibility therefore does not require a node–link representation. A visualization of derived similarities or spatial relationships may qualify when those relationships have an analytic role.

E3—Interactive visual-analytics component. Humans must use an interactive or analytically meaningful visual representation to inspect, understand, validate, steer, compare, interpret, or act on data or computational results. Visual material used solely for static illustration, presentation, communication, or artistic output does not qualify. A mention of visualization without sufficient description of its analytic role leaves this criterion unresolved.

E4—Substantive computational assistance. At least one computational mechanism must contribute nontrivially within the interactive visual-analytics workflow. It may transform or organize the analytic substrate, adapt interaction, recommend or initiate an action, mediate an explanation, or exploit properties of an immersive environment as assistance. Examples include clustering, dimensionality reduction, graph computation, layout, aggregation, annotation, ranking, prediction, natural-language analytic operations, and workflow orchestration. Ordinary rendering, pan, zoom, click, literal manual selection, direct parameter entry, and ordinary database lookup alone do not qualify. Terms such as “AI,” “adaptive,” or “agentic” are insufficient without evidence of the mechanism’s behavior and analytic role.

E5—Human analytic relationship. Humans must meaningfully inspect, direct, validate, interpret, collaborate with, supervise, correct, or make decisions from the assisted computational process. Fully offline or automated computation without a meaningful human analytic relationship to its results is excluded. The criterion concerns the human role in analysis, rather than requiring manual control over every operation.

E6—Administrative scope. Eligible document types are journal, conference, workshop, and preprint research papers with English-language full text available for review. There is no lower publication-date cutoff; the upper boundary is the documented retrieval end date of the regenerated search run. A survey or conceptual paper qualifies only when it independently reports or analyzes a system satisfying E1–E5. Publication community is not an additional eligibility restriction.

E7—Evidence sufficiency. The reviewed evidence must identify at least one candidate system and support a defensible determination for E1–E6. Insufficient title, abstract, or metadata evidence leaves a record uncertain and requires full-text escalation. Insufficient evidence becomes an exclusion only after documented escalation and human review where required. Retrieval or parsing failure alone does not establish ineligibility.

Each criterion receives a Yes, No, or Uncertain decision supported by the reviewed evidence. A paper is eligible when all seven criteria are Yes, excluded when at least one is conclusively No, and uncertain when no criterion is No but at least one remains unresolved. The same rules apply across retrieval sources, assistance modes, tasks, and visualization environments. Prior corpus membership and historical relevance scores do not determine current eligibility.

Eligibility is also distinct from the priority assigned during synthesis. Core papers provide central evidence about the qualifying system and assistance relationship; Supporting papers materially inform a synthesis question but offer narrower or more limited comparative evidence; Contextual papers primarily supply in-scope historical, framing, or evaluation context. All three priorities refer to eligible papers. General frameworks, surveys, and transferable studies that do not satisfy the criteria may inform the review as background or seed evidence, but remain outside the formal corpus and its counts.
