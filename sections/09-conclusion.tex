\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Systems supporting related life-science tasks differ in the computational work they perform and the decisions scientists retain. Comparing them across analytic tasks reveals consequential distinctions: configuring a pipeline versus teaching a model through labeled examples, organizing a representation versus optimizing its layout, interrogating predictions versus assessing evidence for an interpretation. These comparisons identify different evaluation targets: pipeline execution and label propagation, representational changes and layout quality, or the evidence supporting an interpretation. Across all these arrangements, what matters is whether scientists can inspect, correct, and interpret the results.

Taken together, these comparisons answer the four research questions. The corpus is resolved into five recurring task groups: navigation, selection, comparison, sensemaking, and collaboration (Section \ref{sec:tasks}). Four overlapping assistance modes---algorithmic, adaptive, conversational, and immersive---participate in those tasks and redistribute initiative and control between scientists and computation (Section \ref{sec:assistance}), and the detailed comparisons show that superficially similar systems assign different verification responsibilities to scientists (Section \ref{sec:modality}). Visualization modalities condition this assistance through the spatial and collaborative context they provide: what a wall, headset, or desktop makes inspectable changes what the scientist must verify (Sections \ref{sec:modality} and \ref{sec:challenges-continuity}). Finally, evaluation targets differ by arrangement---pipeline execution, representational fidelity, or the evidence supporting an interpretation---and existing studies rarely compare against human-only or autonomous baselines (Section \ref{sec:evaluation}).

These claims are bounded by the eligibility criteria that produced the corpus. Because E4 requires substantive computational assistance, every reviewed system contributes computationally beyond rendering and navigation, which is what makes the four-mode analysis possible. Because E5 requires a human analytic relationship, every placement records decisions scientists retain, which is what makes agency analyzable. And because E1 restricts the corpus to life-science applications, the agenda in Section 8.7 calls for shared life-science scenarios rather than generic benchmarks.

The Assistance $\times$ Visualization Modality framework organizes these mechanisms and environments, while the task groups and locus of analytic agency connect them to analytical purposes and decisions. This organization aims to help researchers compare design alternatives and identify evaluation requirements appropriate to the work supported or delegated. The resulting agenda emphasizes revisable delegation, inspectable transformations, traceable evidence, collaborative common ground, and continuity of analytical context across environments. Progress from hairballs to hypotheses is meaningful when scientists can explain what an assisted analysis suggests and why its evidence warrants the next question.
