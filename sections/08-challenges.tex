\section{Research Challenges and Opportunities}
\label{sec:challenges}
The evaluation evidence in Section 7 identifies several tensions that shape the practical value of computational assistance. Reducing interaction effort can increase the work of supervision. Simplifying a representation can obscure relationships needed for interpretation. Recommendations can help scientists find relevant evidence, while also influencing which alternatives they consider. These tensions concern how assistance participates in scientific work and how its consequences remain open to examination.

We organize the research agenda around these tensions, bringing each challenge together with the opportunity it creates. The five task groups identify the work at stake, while the locus of analytic agency directs attention to who determines the purpose, selects an operation, and assesses its result. Assistance modes and visualization environments condition how these responsibilities can be exercised. Each direction in the following connects an observed limitation to a design question and the evidence needed to assess a proposed solution.

\subsection{Delegation and Analyst Control}
\plan{How can delegation coexist with inspectable scope, interruption, correction, and recovery? Relate control requirements to operation consequences and the practical cost of exercising them.}

Delegation can reduce the effort to perform an analysis, but the scientist’s question can change while the system is working. A sequence that initially matches the intended comparison may become inappropriate when an analyst notices poor data coverage, revises an exclusion criterion, or turns to a different explanation. The challenge is to make the scope of delegated work understandable and revisable throughout execution. Requiring approval for every action can make delegation burdensome, while approval of an initial plan may provide little control over decisions made later.

ProactiveVA illustrates this distinction. Its users could adjust the frequency and categories of proactive guidance, yet a participant requested the ability to intervene during execution because the agent progressed too quickly. The authors identify finer control over ongoing agent activity as an unresolved limitation. This observation separates control over when assistance appears from control over how the resulting analysis proceeds. It motivates a design question: where should a system pause, expose a consequential choice, or allow an analyst to revise the remaining work? Zhao et al.

For example, a scientist comparing expression patterns across two conditions might delegate the calculation and ranking of candidate differences while retaining control over cohort definitions and exclusion criteria. If the scientist changes a cohort after inspecting a heatmap, the system needs to identify which comparisons depend on the earlier definition and make their status clear. Continuing with the old cohort would violate the revised purpose; restarting every operation could discard useful work. This illustrative case suggests an opportunity to represent delegation through explicit inputs, constraints, and dependencies, so that analysts can revise a scientific decision and see which operations require reconsideration.

Intervention points should therefore reflect the consequences of an operation. Recomputing a view under established parameters may require little supervision; changing the population being compared or the criterion used to rank candidates may warrant an explicit check. The appropriate arrangement can also change as an analyst gains experience or encounters unfamiliar data. Algorithmic execution, adaptive recommendations, and conversational orchestration all raise this question, even when the same interface supports them. Across desktop and immersive environments, the analyst must be able to inspect the affected data and criteria without losing the context needed to judge the change. An interruption control is useful only if the analyst can understand what is being interrupted and what remains valid afterward.

For Selection, Filtering, and Precision Interaction and Comparison and Differentiation, this direction calls for studies in which goals or constraints change during delegated work. Building on Section 7’s control and recovery criteria, evaluations could compare approval of a complete plan, approval of individual operations, and checkpoints at consequential decisions. Relevant outcomes include whether analysts recognize a mismatch, successfully redirect the work, preserve valid intermediate results, and incur less total supervision and correction effort. For Sensemaking and Hypothesis Development, the further question is whether scientists can reconstruct how the revised process supports an interpretation. The opportunity is to make delegation adjustable as inquiry develops, with the scientist retaining responsibility for the resulting claim.

\subsection{Compression and Representational Fidelity}
\plan{How can abstraction reduce complexity while exposing what it removes or distorts? Connect multiscale representations with uncertainty inspection and the biological claims they support.}


Reducing visual complexity makes large biological datasets more tractable, but it also changes the evidence available for inspection. Here, compression refers to the reduction of the detail presented through filtering, aggregation, abstraction, or approximation. A representation may preserve broad structure while obscuring a small subgroup or a relationship that matters to the scientific question. The challenge is to make this reduction useful without making its consequences difficult to recognize or investigate.

Edge bundling provides a concrete example. Holten’s method brings related edges together to reveal broader structure, while the strength of bundling affects the visibility of individual connections. The resulting ambiguity in following edges illustrates why an improvement in overview does not establish that a representation supports detailed tracing equally well. [Holten, 2006](https://www.cs.jhu.edu/~misha/ReadingSeminar/Papers/Holten06.pdf) Section 7.2 treats this distinction as an evaluation problem. The corresponding design opportunity is to connect a simplified view to the detail and transformation choices needed to examine a particular interpretation.

Consider a heatmap that summarizes expression across groups of cells. A similar group average in two experimental conditions could conceal a response confined to a small cell population. Allowing the scientist to expand a group is helpful, but that control alone does not indicate which group warrants closer inspection. In this illustrative case, the summary could expose group size and within-group variation, with access to the contributing observations and grouping criteria. Such information would help scientists judge whether an apparent similarity reflects consistent behavior or an average over different responses. The design question is how to provide these indications without recreating the complexity that aggregation was intended to reduce.

The locus of analytic agency matters because the choice of what to suppress can be made by the scientist, fixed by an algorithm, or adjusted by an adaptive system. If the system changes aggregation or filtering as the analyst navigates, a change in the display may reflect a new representation rather than a new biological relationship. A promising direction is to make the active transformation and its scope visible, preserve correspondence between overview and detail, and allow analysts to hold a representation constant while comparing conditions. These mechanisms would make compression a decision that can be examined and revised during analysis. Their usefulness depends on whether scientists can recognize when the current representation is insufficient for the question at hand.

This direction applies to Navigation and Multiscale Orientation and Comparison and Differentiation across desktop, large-display, and immersive environments. Additional display space or depth cues may change the way detail is inspected, but they cannot recover observations or relationships already removed from the displayed representation. Building on Section 7.2, studies could compare a fixed summary, a summary with access to detail, and a summary that also indicates what may be obscured. Tasks should include both broad patterns and localized differences, measuring whether scientists detect relevant exceptions, distinguish representational effects from biological variation, and revise an interpretation when detail contradicts the overview. Computational measures of preservation remain useful, but the scientific requirement is that simplification leave analysts able to discover when the omitted detail matters.


\subsection{Guidance and Diversity of Exploration}
\plan{How can recommendations help without narrowing inquiry prematurely? Develop alternative suggestions, visible criteria, and evaluation of missed evidence.}

Guidance helps scientists decide where to look next, but selecting a useful next step can also narrow the directions that remain visible. Recommendations based on previous interactions may favor familiar variables, populations, or explanations, making the current line of inquiry easier to pursue. That continuity can be valuable when a scientist is investigating a specific question. The challenge is to support purposeful exploration while preserving opportunities to encounter evidence that could change its direction. Diversity therefore concerns scientifically relevant alternatives, rather than simply increasing the number of views or suggestions presented.

Ha et al. compare user-modeling techniques for predicting data interactions and detecting exploration bias, treating these as distinct capabilities. [Ha et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.05021) Their comparison provides a basis for separating how well a system anticipates an analyst’s behavior from whether guidance improves the resulting inquiry. As discussed in Section 7.4, accurate prediction alone does not establish that the current exploration pattern should be reinforced. The research opportunity is to use interaction history both to support the scientist’s immediate purpose and to make potentially consequential omissions visible. A record of what has received attention does not, by itself, reveal whether an unexplored alternative was deliberately excluded or simply overlooked.

Consider a scientist examining an apparent treatment response in a cell population. A recommender might repeatedly suggest genes with expression patterns similar to those already selected, helping characterize the response in greater detail. Yet those suggestions may contribute little to distinguishing a treatment effect from a difference in cell composition or data quality. In this illustrative case, useful alternatives could include a comparison within matched cell types, inspection of sample-level consistency, or a view of quality measures. Their value lies in testing different explanations for the observed pattern. Presenting more genes from the same pattern would increase the number of candidates without necessarily broadening the reasoning.

One design direction is to make the purpose of a recommendation explicit: whether it extends the current analysis, checks its robustness, or examines a competing explanation. Analysts could compare suggestions generated under different criteria and revise which criteria guide subsequent recommendations. These are possible interaction mechanisms, not additional assistance categories. They apply to algorithmic rankings, adaptive guidance, and conversational suggestions, each of which can influence what receives attention before the scientist accepts an operation. The locus of analytic agency makes this influence explicit: scientists need opportunities to question the selection of possibilities as well as their execution. Those opportunities must remain practical across environments. A desktop list, a shared display, or an immersive attention cue presents alternatives differently, so a suggestion’s visibility and the effort required to inspect it are part of the design problem.

For Comparison and Differentiation and Sensemaking and Hypothesis Development, the central evaluation question is whether guidance helps scientists consider alternatives that matter to their conclusions. Building on Section 7.4, studies could compare guidance that extends the current exploration with guidance that also offers robustness checks or competing explanations, alongside an unassisted baseline. Measures of coverage and exploration diversity should be interpreted together with recognition of conflicting evidence, the justification of conclusions, and the effort spent following unproductive suggestions. Focused examination is not inherently biased, and broader exploration is not automatically better. A useful system should help analysts deepen a justified line of inquiry while recognizing when the evidence calls for another direction.


\subsection{Generative Capability and Scientific Provenance}
\plan{How can explanations and orchestration remain grounded in inspectable evidence? Connect outputs to data, execution records, uncertainty, and validation responsibilities.}

Generative assistance can translate a scientific question into queries, code, visualizations, and proposed explanations. Each translation introduces choices about what the question means and what evidence would answer it. A fluent account of the resulting analysis may leave those choices unclear, particularly when it combines observed patterns with inferred explanations. The challenge is to preserve an inspectable connection between the scientist’s request, the operations actually performed, and the claims made about their results.

PhenoFlow provides a concrete example of why this connection matters. In one case study, neurologists requested a cohort defined partly by treatment timing. Inspection revealed that the dataset lacked the required timing information, and the researchers revised their query. [Kim et al., PhenoFlow](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.16329v1) The case illustrates a limit that successful query generation cannot resolve: a requested distinction may not be supported by the available data. It motivates systems that make unsupported parts of a request explicit and preserve the difference between the original question and the narrower question that can be answered. Revising a query should leave a record of the assumption or missing evidence that prompted the revision.

Section 7.3 distinguishes explanations from verifiable execution traces. The corresponding research opportunity is to connect generated statements to the specific evidence and operations on which they depend. A scientist inspecting a summary should be able to reach the relevant observations, cohort definitions, transformations, and outputs, and distinguish these from external knowledge or a proposed hypothesis. Execution records need to reflect what the system actually ran, including failures and revisions. A generated description of an intended procedure cannot establish that the procedure occurred. Similarly, a citation provides a route to a source, but its presence alone does not establish that the source supports the associated claim.

Consider a system that summarizes a difference between two biological subnetworks and proposes a mechanism that could explain it. The measured difference, the choice of comparison criteria, and the proposed mechanism have different evidential roles. In this illustrative workflow, the interface could let the scientist inspect the calculation separately from the literature supporting the mechanism and identify which part remains hypothetical. If the comparison criteria later change, the system should flag dependent statements for reassessment. This extends provenance from reconstructing operations to maintaining the relationship between results and interpretations as the analysis develops. It also gives practical meaning to the locus of analytic agency: the system may generate an explanation, while the scientist assesses whether its evidence warrants a scientific claim.

These questions are especially relevant to conversational assistance and agentic orchestration supporting Comparison and Differentiation and Sensemaking and Hypothesis Development. Across desktop, large-display, and immersive environments, evidence inspection must remain accessible alongside the generated output; a spoken explanation or spatial annotation also needs a persistent route to its supporting records. Building on Section 7.3, studies could compare narrative explanations alone with explanations linked to execution records and source evidence. Cases should include unavailable data, unsupported interpretations, discrepancies between described and executed operations, and conclusions affected by subsequent revisions. Evaluation should establish whether scientists detect these problems, identify which claims remain supported, and correct the analysis at a practical cost. The opportunity is to make generative capability useful in scientific reasoning by keeping its contributions traceable and its evidential limits visible.


\subsection{Personalization and Collaborative Common Ground}
\plan{How can tailored assistance preserve shared understanding? Examine conflicting recommendations, individual and group objectives, and accountability for shared changes.}

Personalized assistance can help scientists work with different expertise, questions, and responsibilities within the same analysis. One collaborator may need guidance through unfamiliar data, while another benefits from concise suggestions and direct access to parameters. The challenge arises when these tailored contributions affect shared work. Collaborators may see the same result without knowing that they reached it through different filters, recommendations, or assumptions. Common ground therefore requires enough shared understanding of the purpose, state, and basis of an analysis for participants to coordinate and assess its conclusions.

The study reported in *Talk to the Wall* illustrates a related tension between awareness and interruption. Participants described how hearing the speech commands of a partner helped them follow the activity of that partner, while some also expressed concern about distraction. The study examined speech interaction rather than personalized assistance, but it shows how an interaction useful to one participant can also inform or interrupt another. [Molina León et al., Talk to the Wall](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.03813v2) For personalized systems, the corresponding question is which changes and recommendations need to become visible to collaborators, when they should be shared, and how much explanation is needed. Broadcasting every action could make shared awareness costly to maintain; keeping all assistance private could conceal decisions that affect the group.

Consider a biologist and a computational analyst comparing cell populations across experimental conditions. The biologist receives suggestions about populations worth inspecting, while the analyst receives guidance about filtering and normalization. These different forms of assistance could support a useful division of work. However, if a recommended filter changes the observations underlying a shared heatmap, the biologist needs to know that the comparison has changed. In this illustrative case, the relevant shared information includes the filter’s scope, its effect on the displayed populations, and whether the change is exploratory or has been adopted for the joint analysis. The team does not need to receive every private suggestion to understand this consequential change.

A research opportunity is to support explicit transitions between individual exploration and shared analytic decisions. Scientists could explore a recommendation locally, compare its consequences with the current shared state, and present the proposed change with its supporting evidence. Conflicting recommendations should remain attributable to their criteria and context so that collaborators can examine the disagreement. Algorithmic rankings, adaptive guidance, and conversational assistance can each contribute to these conflicts. Through the lens of analytic agency, a request from one participant establishes that participant’s intent; it does not necessarily establish the group’s agreement. Systems should make clear who proposed a shared change, whose work it affects, and how collaborators can question or revise it.

For Coordination and Collaborative Reasoning and Sensemaking and Hypothesis Development, this direction calls for evaluation across periods of separate and joint work. Building on Section 7.6, studies could compare shared assistance, private personalized assistance, and personalized assistance with explicit mechanisms for sharing consequential changes. Desktop windows, shared wall displays, and personal immersive views provide different opportunities to observe a partner’s activity, so access to recommendations and state changes should be documented. Relevant outcomes include whether collaborators recognize divergent assumptions, resolve conflicting changes, and explain the evidence behind a joint conclusion, alongside interruption costs and the distribution of participation. The aim is to preserve the benefits of specialized assistance while making the decisions that shape shared findings available for collective scrutiny.


\subsection{Modality Specialization and Workflow Continuity}
\plan{How can environment-specific strengths be used without losing state or provenance? Connect spatially aware reasoning and hybrid workflows to synchronization, correction, and accessibility.}

A scientific workflow can use different environments for different parts of the analysis: spatial inspection in an immersive view, parameter editing on a desktop, and discussion around a shared display. The value of this arrangement depends on what survives the transition. A selected structure, comparison, or annotation must remain connected to the same data and analytic history even when its appearance and interaction controls change. The challenge is to use the strengths of each environment while preserving the information needed to understand, continue, and correct the work.

DashSpace provides an architectural example through a collaborative workspace spanning desktop and immersive devices, with support for shared state and offline editing followed by synchronization. Its discussion of offline use distinguishes automatically handled low-level conflicts from higher-level semantic conflicts, for which user support remains future work. [Borowski et al., DashSpace](https://pure.au.dk/ws/files/418542389/TVCG_2025_DashSpace_Draft.pdf) This distinction matters for scientific analysis: synchronizing application state does not establish that independently made changes remain analytically consistent. A shared workspace can distribute updated values while leaving collaborators to determine whether the resulting comparison still answers their question.

Consider a scientist selecting a tissue region in VR and continuing its analysis through a desktop expression matrix. In this illustrative workflow, the selection needs to retain the identities of the observations, the spatial extent of the region, and the filters under which it was defined. A conversational request to inspect “these cells” must refer to that recorded selection, rather than whichever objects happen to occupy a similar screen position after the transition. Spatially aware assistance therefore needs to connect pointing, gaze, or verbal references to identifiable data and the relevant view state. If the selection is ambiguous, the analyst needs a way to inspect and correct its interpretation before it becomes the input to subsequent operations.

This continuity also depends on distributed input: allocating interaction across devices or input channels within a workflow. A scientist might select a region with a tracked controller, refine a threshold with a keyboard, and inspect the result on a shared wall. These actions need a common account of what was selected, changed, and accepted, even though their physical execution differs. A research opportunity is to preserve data identities, transformation parameters, annotations, and operation history across these transitions while allowing each environment to present suitable controls. Concurrent or delayed changes should expose consequential conflicts, such as an annotation referring to a selection that another participant has redefined. Correction should preserve enough history to explain which state supported an earlier interpretation.

Through the lens of analytic agency, changing environments should leave the allocation of decisions understandable. If assistance adjusts a viewpoint, simplifies a representation, or interprets a spatial reference during a transition, that contribution should remain inspectable. Accessibility is part of this requirement: a collaborator who cannot use a headset or a particular input technique still needs a practical way to inspect evidence, question a selection, and revise an operation. Different environments need not offer identical interactions, but they should preserve access to the decisions that matter to the shared scientific task.

For Navigation and Multiscale Orientation, Selection, Filtering, and Precision Interaction, and Coordination and Collaborative Reasoning, evaluation should follow complete transitions rather than assess each environment only in isolation. Building on Sections 7.3, 7.5, and 7.6, studies could compare a workflow within one environment with a distributed workflow, recording both task benefits and the cost of reconstructing context. Relevant outcomes include preservation of selections and parameters, recognition of stale or conflicting states, correction success, reorientation effort, and participation through alternative input methods. The opportunity is to make specialization across environments useful without requiring scientists to rebuild the analysis or surrender opportunities for oversight whenever they change devices.
