The locus of analytic agency connects these perspectives by examining how initiative and control are distributed within a workflow.

\label{subsection:locusofagency}

We use *locus of analytic agency* to describe who takes the initiative and who controls decisions during visual analysis. This can change as a workflow proceeds. A scientist may define a question, ask a system to identify promising structures, revise its suggestions, and assess their biological meaning. Holter and El-Assady (§4.1) describe agency in terms of control over the analysis process and responsibility for decision-making. They also discuss how control can depend on the stage of interaction. Building on this perspective, we examine how scientists and computational systems share decisions within an analytic workflow.

Different parts of a task can involve different divisions of work. An analyst who requests a comparison may let the system select candidates and calculate differences while retaining control over the comparison criteria and interpretation. Monadjemi et al. (§§5.1–5.2) examine how humans and artificial agents contribute to shared analytic tasks, including setting constraints, recommending actions, and contributing knowledge. For this review, we ask who proposes an operation, who chooses its scope and parameters, who carries it out, and who assesses the result. We also ask whether the analyst can inspect, change, or undo what the system has done. These questions guide our comparison of workflows.

Consider an illustrative comparison of biological subnetworks across two experimental conditions. The scientist identifies the question and datasets. The system ranks candidate subnetworks using a chosen measure of similarity or difference. The scientist examines the candidates, excludes one with poor data coverage, and adjusts the comparison criteria. The system then calculates and displays the differences. Finally, the scientist considers whether the pattern reflects biological variation, measurement limitations, or choices made during analysis. Here, the scientist sets the purpose, the system helps determine which structures receive attention, and the scientist revises the comparison and interprets the findings. If the system suggests a follow-up comparison, it also helps determine what happens next.

This example distinguishes carrying out an operation, proposing the next step, and judging what a result means. A calculation may be fully automated even though the scientist has chosen its purpose, inputs, and parameters. A recommendation can influence the direction of analysis even when the scientist must approve it. Monadjemi et al. (§5.1) observe that recommendations may indirectly shape analytic goals; they also emphasize that mixed initiative need not involve high levels of automation (§5.4). In scientific analysis, a further question is whether the evidence supports the resulting interpretation. A system may suggest a biological explanation, but that suggestion still needs to be checked against the data and the assumptions behind the analysis. We therefore examine what opportunities analysts have to check results, consider alternatives, and correct earlier decisions. An approval button alone does not tell us whether those opportunities are available.

This perspective connects the tasks, assistance modes, and visualization modalities considered in the review. Tasks describe what analysts want to accomplish. Assistance modes describe how computation helps. Modalities describe the environments through which analysts inspect information and interact with the system. Moving a comparison from a desktop to an immersive display may leave the division of decisions unchanged. Introducing system-recommended candidates may change that division in either environment. The following sections examine who directs the work, what the system contributes, what analysts can question or change, and what evaluations show about the consequences.
