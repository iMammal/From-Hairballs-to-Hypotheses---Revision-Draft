\section{Evaluation of Assisted Visual Analytics}

\label{sec:evaluation}

Evaluation tests whether computational assistance improves scientific analysis, and under what conditions. A system may reduce the time needed to select observations, improve the accuracy of a comparison, or help a scientist develop an explanation. These outcomes support different claims. Faster interaction alone does not establish a better interpretation, and a plausible explanation still requires evidence. Isenberg et al.'s review of visualization evaluation distinguishes goals that include computational performance, user performance, and understanding analytic work. These distinctions help separate the performance of individual operations from their consequences for the broader scientific workflow.~\cite{EvalIsenberg2013}

The five task categories introduced in Section 3 help identify which outcomes matter. Navigation and Multiscale Orientation concerns finding relevant information while maintaining context. Selection, Filtering, and Precision Interaction concerns identifying and manipulating the intended observations. Comparison and Differentiation concerns recognizing and assessing meaningful relationships. Sensemaking and Hypothesis Development extends this assessment to explanations and their supporting evidence. Coordination and Collaborative Reasoning concerns how scientists establish shared understanding and combine their contributions. Evaluation should relate a computational intervention to the task it supports and to the judgments that depend on its output.

The locus of analytic agency helps identify what needs to be evaluated within that task. When a system selects candidates, recommends a view, or carries out a sequence of operations, evaluation should examine both the resulting output and the scientist's ability to assess and direct that contribution. Relevant questions include whether analysts recognize errors, understand the basis of a recommendation, consider alternatives, and recover from an unsuitable intervention. The effort required to specify, inspect, and correct assistance is also part of its practical value.

This section organizes evaluation around dimensions that apply across assistance modes and visualization environments. For each dimension, we examine the claim being tested, the evidence reported, and the conditions that limit its interpretation. Controlled comparisons, computational benchmarks, expert assessments, and observations of sustained use answer different questions. We consider their relevance to the claims made, while distinguishing demonstrated outcomes from capabilities or benefits that remain unevaluated.

\subsection{Analytic Effectiveness and Scientific Utility}

\label{sec:evaluation-effectiveness}

Analytic effectiveness depends on what the scientist is trying to accomplish. For selection, useful outcomes include identifying the intended observations accurately and with manageable correction effort. For comparison, success includes recognizing the relevant differences and understanding the basis on which they were calculated. For hypothesis development, evaluation must also consider whether an explanation follows from the evidence and whether alternatives have been examined. Speed and accuracy on a bounded task provide useful evidence, but their implications for scientific reasoning depend on the task and its place in the workflow.

The choice of baseline determines which contribution can be assessed. Poco et al.'s work on multidimensional projections shows that desktop 3D views can support more reliable and confident responses for some tasks, while also introducing interaction difficulties.~\cite{EvalPoco2011} This motivates comparisons with capable desktop alternatives when evaluating immersive environments. If the display, representation, and interaction technique all change together, an observed advantage cannot be attributed to immersion alone. Similarly, success in identifying a cluster or comparing distances does not establish that the structure has a valid biological interpretation.

Assistance can be examined more directly by comparing the same interface with and without the relevant computational intervention, or by comparing alternative assistance strategies under equivalent task conditions. Interpretation requires information about the participants and their expertise, the datasets, the baseline, and the duration of use. A method that helps an unfamiliar user complete a prescribed comparison may have different value for a domain expert pursuing an evolving question. Computational scale and response latency also affect which tasks remain practical, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation-workload}.

Therefore, we distinguish evidence of feasibility, controlled task performance, expert feedback, and sustained scientific use. A demonstration can establish that an operation is possible. A controlled study can test a defined performance claim. Expert assessment can reveal whether outputs and interactions address domain needs, while observations of sustained use can show how assistance fits into an extended workflow. These forms of evidence are complementary. A domain case may demonstrate a useful application without isolating the causal contribution of assistance, and favorable feedback does not substitute for checking the biological claim produced through the analysis.

\subsection{Representational Fidelity and Uncertainty}

\label{sec:evaluation-fidelity}

Computational assistance can change the information available for interpretation through filtering, aggregation, layout, embedding, or approximation. Representational fidelity concerns whether those changes preserve the relationships needed for the task. Relevant properties may include network topology, neighborhood structure, spatial relationships, or differences across scales. A clearer overview may be useful even when it suppresses detail, but evaluation should identify what was preserved, what was removed, and which judgments depend on the missing information.

Edge bundling illustrates this trade-off. Holten's method groups related links to reduce clutter, with bundling strength affecting the balance between individual connections and broader structure. The paper also discusses ambiguity in following bundled edges; its informal feedback does not establish improved biological interpretation.~\cite{EvalHolten2006} CMGV addresses a different part of complexity management through operations such as filtering, hiding, and collapsing graph elements, maintaining distinct underlying and visible graphs. Its integrity and performance tests assess the behavior of those operations, rather than scientists' understanding of the resulting views.~\cite{EvalZafar2026} Together, these examples distinguish correctness of a transformation from the consequences of presenting its output.

Computational fidelity and interpretive fidelity require different evidence. A neighborhood-preservation score can measure a property of an embedding, but does not show whether an analyst recognizes where that embedding is misleading. Consider an expression heatmap that summarizes cells by population. The summary may correctly represent each population's mean while concealing a small subgroup with a contrasting pattern. Evaluation could examine both the accuracy of the aggregation and whether scientists recognize the need to inspect within-population variation. The appropriate level of detail depends on the scientific question, rather than on a universal preference for either overview or precision.

Uncertainty in the observations and inferences should also be distinguished from distortion introduced by the representation. Missing measurements, uncertain relationships, and unstable estimates raise different questions from overlap, projection distortion, or omitted detail. A display can accurately present uncertain data, while a visually clear transformation can conceal important limitations. Evaluation should therefore examine whether scientists can identify the relevant uncertainty, understand its implications for a comparison, and judge when additional evidence is required.

The allocation of analytic decisions matters when the system chooses which structures to retain or which transformations to apply. Evaluation should examine whether analysts can inspect those choices, compare alternatives, and return to the source observations. For Navigation and Multiscale Orientation, this includes maintaining the relationship between local detail and broader context. For Comparison and Differentiation and Sensemaking and Hypothesis Development, it includes recognizing when a visible pattern depends on a filtering rule, aggregation level, or parameter choice. The records needed to make those checks lead directly to provenance and recovery.


\subsection{Provenance and Recovery}

\label{sec:evaluation-provenance}

Provenance records how an analytic result was produced. In assisted analysis, relevant records include the inputs, transformations, parameters, recommendations, and delegated operations that led to the current state. Their practical value depends on whether scientists can use them to answer questions about the result. A detailed log may establish that an operation occurred without making its consequences easy to inspect. Evaluation should therefore distinguish the availability of records from usable auditability: the ability to trace a result to the decisions and operations responsible for it.

VisTrails provides an established basis for this distinction through its treatment of scientific workflows and their versions. Freire et al.~describe recording changes so that analysts can return to previous workflows, modify them, and compare alternatives. This supports reconstruction of the analytic process as it evolves. The capability does not, by itself, establish that users will detect an error or select an appropriate correction, but it provides the history on which those actions can depend.~\cite{EvalFreire2006}

Conversational assistance introduces another distinction between an explanation of an operation and evidence of its execution. DeepVIS exposes intermediate steps in translating a natural-language request into a visualization and allows targeted refinement. Its user study reports favorable participant assessments of understanding and refinement, along with separate computational evaluations of visualization generation. These results do not establish recovery from errors in biological analysis.~\cite{EvalShuai2025} More generally, a plausible account of why a system selected particular data is insufficient unless the selected inputs and executed transformations can also be checked. Explanations can help analysts interpret a trace, but should not be treated as substitutes for one.

Recovery extends beyond reversing the most recent action. Consider a filter that inadvertently excludes a relevant cell population before a comparison and summary are generated. Correcting the filter is only part of the repair: the dependent outputs must also be updated, and the scientist must recognize which earlier conclusions require reconsideration. Evaluation could examine whether analysts detect the problem, locate the responsible operation, restore or revise the relevant state, and identify affected results. Recovery success, correction time, and the persistence of errors in downstream outputs provide different views of this process.

Through the lens of analytic agency, provenance makes computational contributions available for inspection, and recovery makes them revisable. These capabilities support Comparison and Differentiation by allowing scientists to examine alternative analytic paths, and Sensemaking and Hypothesis Development by connecting interpretations to their supporting operations and evidence. In collaborative work, provenance should also identify which analyst or system proposed and executed each operation, and whether and by whom its result was accepted. Timestamps and records of prompts or queries, parameters, code, and output help establish what occurred and support subsequent verification and recovery. Whether analysts can inspect and redirect these contributions while assistance is unfolding is the related question of control.

\subsection{Analyst Control, Calibration, and Exploration Bias}

\label{sec:evaluation-control}

Analyst control concerns whether scientists can direct assistance as their questions and understanding change. A system may ask permission before acting, yet leave little opportunity to adjust the steps that follow. Evaluation should therefore examine whether analysts understand the scope of an intervention, can change its direction, and recognize when it no longer serves their purpose. Section~\ref{sec:evaluation-provenance} considered how an analysis can be reconstructed and repaired. Here, the focus is on whether scientists can exercise judgment while assistance shapes the work.

ProactiveVA illustrates the distinction between accepting assistance and controlling its progress. The system infers possible needs from interaction history and offers context-dependent support. Participants requested greater ability to intervene during the process, and the authors identify limited fine-grained steering as an unresolved issue. They also report difficulties when users switch tasks quickly or when noisy interactions obscure intent. These findings, from general visual analytics applications, motivate separate evaluation of whether assistance identifies the right goal and whether users can redirect it. A useful initial suggestion does not establish that subsequent operations remain appropriate.~\cite{EvalZhao2025}

Calibration concerns whether reliance on assistance matches its capabilities and limitations. High trust or frequent acceptance is not sufficient evidence: scientists should be able to use sound suggestions while questioning incorrect or unsupported ones. PhenoFlow makes the difficulty concrete. Its authors report that complex, lengthy explanations could cause neurologists to miss errors, while compressing those explanations could remove necessary information. The system consequently combines explanations with generated code and visual inspection of relevant data fields. This design experience supports examining whether the information provided helps analysts verify a result, rather than assuming that an explanation makes reliance appropriate. Relevant outcomes include acceptance of correct suggestions, rejection or correction of incorrect ones, and recognition that further evidence is needed.~\cite{EvalKim2025}

Appropriate reliance also depends on what a system's demonstrated accuracy actually establishes. Ha et al.~compare user-modeling methods for predicting data interactions and detecting exploration bias. These measures assess how well a system models analytic behavior, but do not establish whether guidance based on those models improves that behavior.~\cite{EvalHa2023} The distinction matters because a system can accurately predict an analyst's behavior while reinforcing an already narrow search. A recommendation may match what the scientist would usually inspect next while overlooking evidence that could challenge the current interpretation. Evaluation must therefore distinguish successful prediction from assistance that helps scientists examine relevant alternatives.

Exploration bias becomes consequential when assistance repeatedly directs attention toward some observations or explanations while leaving relevant alternatives unexamined. Consider a scientist comparing expression patterns across conditions. A recommender that repeatedly surfaces the strongest differences may accelerate the comparison while drawing attention away from a smaller group that challenges the emerging explanation. Evaluation could examine coverage of relevant observations, consideration of competing explanations, and whether important contradictory evidence is found. Greater coverage is not automatically better: focused analysis may be appropriate to the question. The issue is whether assistance narrows inquiry in ways the scientist can recognize and reconsider.

These outcomes should be interpreted in relation to domain expertise, familiarity with the interface, and the stage of analysis. Guidance that helps a scientist learn an unfamiliar workflow may become intrusive once a clear strategy develops. Longer studies can examine whether analysts adjust their reliance as they encounter errors, change goals, or continue without assistance. Through the lens of analytic agency, evaluation asks how system initiative affects the scientist's practical ability to choose the next step. For Navigation and Multiscale Orientation, that includes deciding where to look; for Comparison and Differentiation and Sensemaking and Hypothesis Development, it includes deciding which alternatives deserve further examination.

\subsection{Workload and Embodied Cost}

\label{sec:evaluation-workload}

Assistance can reduce the effort required to perform an operation while increasing the effort required to specify, supervise, or correct it. Evaluation should therefore account for the work needed to reach a usable analytic result. Consider a scientist who requests a cohort comparison through a conversational interface. Generating the views may require fewer interactions than manually constructing them, but the scientist must still check the cohort definitions, inspect the chosen measures, and resolve any misunderstandings. Counting commands or timing generation alone would miss this work. The same concern applies to adaptive recommendations that interrupt an ongoing comparison and algorithmic transformations whose outputs require additional inspection.

Computational performance is part of this accounting. Data loading, preprocessing, layout computation, and updates following an interaction impose different delays. Graphia's comparison with Gephi and Cytoscape illustrates why these should be reported separately: the authors describe differences in rendering responsiveness, but also explain that the layouts were run to different stages of completion, limiting direct comparison of their computation times.~\cite{EvalFreeman2022} Evaluation should therefore record data size and structure, hardware, and the operations included in each timing. A fast display update does not establish that the full analysis is responsive, and a computational benchmark does not by itself establish reduced analyst workload. Where aggregation or approximation improves responsiveness, its representational consequences also belong in the assessment described in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation-fidelity}.

Navigation makes the relationship between interaction and effort especially visible. Analysts may pan across a desktop view, move between regions of a large display, or change position and scale in an immersive environment. Yang et al.~compare room-sized and zoomable 3D scatterplots, each with and without an overview. Their results show task-dependent benefits, which they interpret through the costs of wayfinding, travel, repeated movement, and switching between views.~\cite{EvalYang2021} This provides a useful basis for examining where navigation effort occurs, although the study does not establish the benefit of AI assistance. An assisted viewpoint change could reduce travel while requiring the scientist to recover their orientation or relocate a previous observation. Evaluation should capture both the movement avoided and the work introduced by the transition.

Precision introduces a similar trade-off. Consider assistance that selects a group of cells from a dense spatial view. It may reduce repeated pointing or sustained reaching, yet require careful inspection of the selection boundary and correction of unwanted inclusions. Relevant measures include selection accuracy, correction actions, completion time, and reported effort. Physical effort should be examined wherever the interaction requires it, including touch input on a wall, repeated controller movement, or prolonged mid-air gestures. For immersive workflows, reporting discomfort, breaks, and withdrawals alongside performance helps establish whether an apparent benefit remains usable throughout the intended session. Session duration, input device, and seated or standing use are necessary context for interpreting these outcomes.

These costs should be assessed together without treating them as interchangeable. A shorter task may require more concentration, while a slower workflow may make verification easier. Likewise, fewer interactions may reflect useful automation or insufficient inspection. Comparisons with unassisted or alternative workflows should therefore consider task accuracy, total completion time, correction effort, and reported workload together. Separating time spent specifying, waiting, inspecting, and correcting can reveal whether assistance removes work or transfers it to another stage. This incorporates the supervision costs of conversational and adaptive assistance into the same evaluation frame as algorithmic latency and embodied interaction.

Through the lens of analytic agency, delegation changes both who performs an operation and what work remains for the scientist. The practical benefit depends on whether scientists can use and assess the result with an acceptable level of effort. For Navigation and Multiscale Orientation, this includes maintaining context while changing views; for Selection, Filtering, and Precision Interaction, it includes obtaining and checking the intended subset. In broader sensemaking, time saved in execution must be considered alongside the effort needed to understand what was done and whether it supports the scientific question.

\subsection{Collaboration and Common Ground}

\label{sec:evaluation-collaboration}

Assistance in collaborative analysis changes both the work being performed and what collaborators need to understand about one another's actions. A recommendation may help one scientist identify a useful comparison while leaving others unaware that the selected observations or comparison criteria have changed. Evaluation should therefore examine whether assistance supports common ground: a shared understanding of the objects, operations, and evidence under discussion. Seeing the same visualization is only part of this requirement. Collaborators also need to establish what a selection refers to, which transformations produced the current view, and whether a proposed action represents an individual inquiry or a group decision.

FIESTA illustrates how shared workspaces can support movement between individual exploration and joint analysis. In an exploratory study involving ten groups of three participants, the authors observed teams arranging personal work areas, examining data independently, and coming together to discuss findings. Participants also described the visibility of others' activities as useful for maintaining awareness. These observations identify ways that a shared environment can support coordination; they do not establish that every shared feature improves performance or that teams can successfully supervise autonomous assistance.~\cite{EvalLee2021} For evaluation, the relevant question is whether collaborators can follow changes that affect their work while retaining room for independent investigation.

Speech interaction makes this balance particularly clear. In \emph{Talk to the Wall}, ten pairs used speech and touch during collaborative visual analysis. Fourteen participants commented positively on learning what their partner was doing through spoken commands, while eight reported concern about distracting their partner.~\cite{EvalMolinaLeon2025} Speech can therefore serve two purposes: directing the system and making an action available to collaborators. Its value depends on whether that additional awareness helps coordination or interrupts ongoing work. These findings motivate examining how commands and system responses enter the group's conversation; they do not demonstrate that audible interaction alone produces shared understanding.

Computational initiative adds a further question: whose intent does the system act on? Consider two scientists inspecting a tissue dataset, one examining spatial organization and the other comparing expression profiles. An assistant interprets a request to exclude low-quality cells and updates the shared selection. The operation may satisfy the requester while removing observations that the collaborator was investigating. Evaluation should examine whether the scope of the change was clear, whether the affected collaborator noticed it, and whether the team could question or revise it. A recommendation accepted by one participant should not automatically be treated as evidence of agreement by the group.

Relevant outcomes include the time and clarification needed to establish a shared reference, awareness of consequential changes, successful resolution of conflicting operations, and the ability to trace individual and system contributions. Interaction records and observations of discussion can help explain why a team succeeds or struggles beyond its final completion time and accuracy. Participation also requires careful interpretation. Equal numbers of commands are not necessarily desirable when collaborators have different expertise or responsibilities. The concern is whether each participant can contribute relevant knowledge and challenge an intervention, or whether assistance disproportionately follows the most active speaker or the person controlling the interface.

Through the lens of analytic agency, collaboration distributes initiative and control among several scientists as well as the computational system. Evaluation should examine whether that distribution supports Coordination and Collaborative Reasoning and the scientific judgments that follow. Shared provenance, discussed in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation-provenance}, helps establish who requested an operation and what the system executed. The remaining question is whether collaborators can use that information to reconcile interpretations and assess the evidence together. Agreement is useful for coordinating work, but agreement alone does not establish that a biological explanation is supported.
