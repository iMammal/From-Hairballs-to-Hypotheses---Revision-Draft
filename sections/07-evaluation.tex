\section{Evaluation of Assisted Visual Analytics}

\label{sec:evaluation}

Evaluation tests whether computational assistance improves scientific analysis, and under what conditions. A system may reduce the time needed to select observations, improve the accuracy of a comparison, or help a scientist develop an explanation. These outcomes support different claims. Faster interaction alone does not establish a better interpretation, and a plausible explanation still requires evidence. Isenberg et al.'s review of visualization evaluation distinguishes goals that include computational performance, user performance, and understanding analytic work. These distinctions help separate the performance of individual operations from their consequences for the broader scientific workflow.~\cite{EvalIsenberg2013}

The five task categories introduced in Section 3 help identify which outcomes matter. Navigation and Multiscale Orientation concerns finding relevant information while maintaining context. Selection, Filtering, and Precision Interaction concerns identifying and manipulating the intended observations. Comparison and Differentiation concerns recognizing and assessing meaningful relationships. Sensemaking and Hypothesis Development extends this assessment to explanations and their supporting evidence. Coordination and Collaborative Reasoning concerns how scientists establish shared understanding and combine their contributions. Evaluation should relate a computational intervention to the task it supports and to the judgments that depend on its output.

The locus of analytic agency helps identify what needs to be evaluated within that task. When a system selects candidates, recommends a view, or carries out a sequence of operations, evaluation should examine both the resulting output and the scientist's ability to assess and direct that contribution. Relevant questions include whether analysts recognize errors, understand the basis of a recommendation, consider alternatives, and recover from an unsuitable intervention. The effort required to specify, inspect, and correct assistance is also part of its practical value.

This section organizes evaluation around dimensions that apply across assistance modes and visualization environments. For each dimension, we examine the claim being tested, the evidence reported, and the conditions that limit its interpretation. Controlled comparisons, computational benchmarks, expert assessments, and observations of sustained use answer different questions. We consider their relevance to the claims made, while distinguishing demonstrated outcomes from capabilities or benefits that remain unevaluated.

\subsection{Analytic Effectiveness and Scientific Utility}

\label{sec:evaluation-effectiveness}

Analytic effectiveness depends on what the scientist is trying to accomplish. For selection, useful outcomes include identifying the intended observations accurately and with manageable correction effort. For comparison, success includes recognizing the relevant differences and understanding the basis on which they were calculated. For hypothesis development, evaluation must also consider whether an explanation follows from the evidence and whether alternatives have been examined. Speed and accuracy on a bounded task provide useful evidence, but their implications for scientific reasoning depend on the task and its place in the workflow.

The choice of baseline determines which contribution can be assessed. Poco et al.'s work on multidimensional projections shows that desktop 3D views can support more reliable and confident responses for some tasks, while also introducing interaction difficulties.~\cite{EvalPoco2011} This motivates comparisons with capable desktop alternatives when evaluating immersive environments. If the display, representation, and interaction technique all change together, an observed advantage cannot be attributed to immersion alone. Similarly, success in identifying a cluster or comparing distances does not establish that the structure has a valid biological interpretation.

Assistance can be examined more directly by comparing the same interface with and without the relevant computational intervention, or by comparing alternative assistance strategies under equivalent task conditions. Interpretation requires information about the participants and their expertise, the datasets, the baseline, and the duration of use. A method that helps an unfamiliar user complete a prescribed comparison may have different value for a domain expert pursuing an evolving question. Computational scale and response latency also affect which tasks remain practical, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation-workload}.

Therefore, we distinguish evidence of feasibility, controlled task performance, expert feedback, and sustained scientific use. A demonstration can establish that an operation is possible. A controlled study can test a defined performance claim. Expert assessment can reveal whether outputs and interactions address domain needs, while observations of sustained use can show how assistance fits into an extended workflow. These forms of evidence are complementary. A domain case may demonstrate a useful application without isolating the causal contribution of assistance, and favorable feedback does not substitute for checking the biological claim produced through the analysis.

\subsection{Representational Fidelity and Uncertainty}

\label{sec:evaluation-fidelity}

Computational assistance can change the information available for interpretation through filtering, aggregation, layout, embedding, or approximation. Representational fidelity concerns whether those changes preserve the relationships needed for the task. Relevant properties may include network topology, neighborhood structure, spatial relationships, or differences across scales. A clearer overview may be useful even when it suppresses detail, but evaluation should identify what was preserved, what was removed, and which judgments depend on the missing information.

Edge bundling illustrates this trade-off. Holten's method groups related links to reduce clutter, with bundling strength affecting the balance between individual connections and broader structure. The paper also discusses ambiguity in following bundled edges; its informal feedback does not establish improved biological interpretation.~\cite{EvalHolten2006} CMGV addresses a different part of complexity management through operations such as filtering, hiding, and collapsing graph elements, maintaining distinct underlying and visible graphs. Its integrity and performance tests assess the behavior of those operations, rather than scientists' understanding of the resulting views.~\cite{EvalZafar2026} Together, these examples distinguish correctness of a transformation from the consequences of presenting its output.

Computational fidelity and interpretive fidelity require different evidence. A neighborhood-preservation score can measure a property of an embedding, but does not show whether an analyst recognizes where that embedding is misleading. Consider an expression heatmap that summarizes cells by population. The summary may correctly represent each population's mean while concealing a small subgroup with a contrasting pattern. Evaluation could examine both the accuracy of the aggregation and whether scientists recognize the need to inspect within-population variation. The appropriate level of detail depends on the scientific question, rather than on a universal preference for either overview or precision.

Uncertainty in the observations and inferences should also be distinguished from distortion introduced by the representation. Missing measurements, uncertain relationships, and unstable estimates raise different questions from overlap, projection distortion, or omitted detail. A display can accurately present uncertain data, while a visually clear transformation can conceal important limitations. Evaluation should therefore examine whether scientists can identify the relevant uncertainty, understand its implications for a comparison, and judge when additional evidence is required.

The allocation of analytic decisions matters when the system chooses which structures to retain or which transformations to apply. Evaluation should examine whether analysts can inspect those choices, compare alternatives, and return to the source observations. For Navigation and Multiscale Orientation, this includes maintaining the relationship between local detail and broader context. For Comparison and Differentiation and Sensemaking and Hypothesis Development, it includes recognizing when a visible pattern depends on a filtering rule, aggregation level, or parameter choice. The records needed to make those checks lead directly to provenance and recovery.


\subsection{Provenance and Recovery}

\label{sec:evaluation-provenance}

Provenance records how an analytic result was produced. In assisted analysis, relevant records include the inputs, transformations, parameters, recommendations, and delegated operations that led to the current state. Their practical value depends on whether scientists can use them to answer questions about the result. A detailed log may establish that an operation occurred without making its consequences easy to inspect. Evaluation should therefore distinguish the availability of records from usable auditability: the ability to trace a result to the decisions and operations responsible for it.

VisTrails provides an established basis for this distinction through its treatment of scientific workflows and their versions. Freire et al.~describe recording changes so that analysts can return to previous workflows, modify them, and compare alternatives. This supports reconstruction of the analytic process as it evolves. The capability does not, by itself, establish that users will detect an error or select an appropriate correction, but it provides the history on which those actions can depend.~\cite{EvalFreire2006}

Conversational assistance introduces another distinction between an explanation of an operation and evidence of its execution. DeepVIS exposes intermediate steps in translating a natural-language request into a visualization and allows targeted refinement. Its user study reports favorable participant assessments of understanding and refinement, along with separate computational evaluations of visualization generation. These results do not establish recovery from errors in biological analysis.~\cite{EvalShuai2025} More generally, a plausible account of why a system selected particular data is insufficient unless the selected inputs and executed transformations can also be checked. Explanations can help analysts interpret a trace, but should not be treated as substitutes for one.

Recovery extends beyond reversing the most recent action. Consider a filter that inadvertently excludes a relevant cell population before a comparison and summary are generated. Correcting the filter is only part of the repair: the dependent outputs must also be updated, and the scientist must recognize which earlier conclusions require reconsideration. Evaluation could examine whether analysts detect the problem, locate the responsible operation, restore or revise the relevant state, and identify affected results. Recovery success, correction time, and the persistence of errors in downstream outputs provide different views of this process.

Through the lens of analytic agency, provenance makes computational contributions available for inspection and recovery makes them revisable. These capabilities support Comparison and Differentiation by allowing scientists to examine alternative analytic paths, and Sensemaking and Hypothesis Development by connecting interpretations to their supporting operations and evidence. In collaborative work, records must also distinguish individual and system contributions. Whether analysts can exercise these opportunities while assistance is unfolding is the related question of control.
