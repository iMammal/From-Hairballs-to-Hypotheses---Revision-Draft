\section{Evaluation of Assisted Visual Analytics}

\label{sec:evaluation}

Evaluation tests whether computational assistance improves scientific analysis, and under what conditions. A system may reduce the time needed to select observations, improve the accuracy of a comparison, or help a scientist develop an explanation. These outcomes support different claims. Faster interaction does not by itself establish better interpretation, and a plausible explanation still requires evidence. Isenberg et al.'s review of visualization evaluation distinguishes goals that include computational performance, user performance, and understanding analytic work. These distinctions help separate the performance of individual operations from their consequences for the broader scientific workflow.~\cite{EvalIsenberg2013}

The five task categories introduced in Section 3 help identify which outcomes matter. Navigation and Multiscale Orientation concerns finding relevant information while maintaining context. Selection, Filtering, and Precision Interaction concerns identifying and manipulating the intended observations. Comparison and Differentiation concerns recognizing and assessing meaningful relationships. Sensemaking and Hypothesis Development extends this assessment to explanations and their supporting evidence. Coordination and Collaborative Reasoning concerns how scientists establish shared understanding and combine their contributions. Evaluation should relate a computational intervention to the task it supports and to the judgments that depend on its output.

The locus of analytic agency helps identify what needs to be evaluated within that task. When a system selects candidates, recommends a view, or carries out a sequence of operations, evaluation should examine both the resulting output and the scientist's ability to assess and direct that contribution. Relevant questions include whether analysts recognize errors, understand the basis of a recommendation, consider alternatives, and recover from an unsuitable intervention. The effort required to specify, inspect, and correct assistance is also part of its practical value.

This section organizes evaluation around dimensions that apply across assistance modes and visualization environments. For each dimension, we examine the claim being tested, the evidence reported, and the conditions that limit its interpretation. Controlled comparisons, computational benchmarks, expert assessments, and observations of sustained use answer different questions. We consider their relevance to the claims made, while distinguishing demonstrated outcomes from capabilities or benefits that remain unevaluated.

\subsection{Analytic Effectiveness and Scientific Utility}

\label{sec:evaluation-effectiveness}

Analytic effectiveness depends on what the scientist is trying to accomplish. For selection, useful outcomes include identifying the intended observations accurately and with manageable correction effort. For comparison, success includes recognizing the relevant differences and understanding the basis on which they were calculated. For hypothesis development, evaluation must also consider whether an explanation follows from the evidence and whether alternatives have been examined. Speed and accuracy on a bounded task provide useful evidence, but their implications for scientific reasoning depend on the task and its place in the workflow.

The choice of baseline determines which contribution can be assessed. Poco et al.'s work on multidimensional projections shows that desktop 3D views can support more reliable and confident responses for some tasks, while also introducing interaction difficulties.~\cite{EvalPoco2011} This motivates comparisons with capable desktop alternatives when evaluating immersive environments. If the display, representation, and interaction technique all change together, an observed advantage cannot be attributed to immersion alone. Likewise, success in identifying a cluster or comparing distances does not establish that the structure has a valid biological interpretation.

Assistance can be examined more directly by comparing the same interface with and without the relevant computational intervention, or by comparing alternative assistance strategies under equivalent task conditions. Interpretation requires information about the participants and their expertise, the datasets, the baseline, and the duration of use. A method that helps an unfamiliar user complete a prescribed comparison may have different value for a domain expert pursuing an evolving question. Computational scale and response latency also affect which tasks remain practical, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation-workload}.

We therefore distinguish evidence of feasibility, controlled task performance, expert feedback, and sustained scientific use. A demonstration can establish that an operation is possible. A controlled study can test a defined performance claim. Expert assessment can reveal whether outputs and interactions address domain needs, while observations of sustained use can show how assistance fits into an extended workflow. These forms of evidence are complementary. A domain case may demonstrate a useful application without isolating the causal contribution of assistance, and favorable feedback does not substitute for checking the biological claim produced through the analysis.
